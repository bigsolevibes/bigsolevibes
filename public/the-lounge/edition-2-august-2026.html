### The Standard, Assembled

The alarm went off at 5:47. He'd set it for 5:45. He let it go twice before the third ring, then sat up.

This is not a story about discipline. Most men who are up at 5:47 are not doing it for the story. They are doing it because there is a thing they want to stay ahead of — a body that has opinions, a day that fills itself if you don't fill it first, a mirror that reports back without commentary. He had learned, over several years and one visit to a physical therapist who looked at the bottoms of his feet the way a mechanic looks at worn brake pads, that the morning was structural. You either built it or it just happened to you.

He drank water. Then he went to the bathroom.

The shower is where the morning's tone gets set. He had used plenty of body washes that smelled fine, did the job, disappeared. Then he'd tried the **Baxter of California Invigorating Body Wash Citrus Herbal Musk** — citrus up front, cedar underneath, musk that doesn't announce itself so much as confirm something. He'd gone through three bottles. The fourth was under the sink. You don't let yourself run out of the thing that means the morning started right.

He reached for the bar. The **Baxter of California Exfoliating Body Bar Cedarwood Oak Moss** had changed his thinking about bar soap, which he had previously filed under *adequate*. This one had actual grit worked into it — cedarwood and oak moss on the nose, something grainy in the lather. He'd noticed it first in how his forearms looked in August, then in how everything just handled better. Some improvements don't announce themselves. You just stop having the problem.

He dried off. Stood in front of the mirror with the particular neutrality of a man who has made his peace with the face looking back.

The cologne was on the left side of the shelf, where it had been for four months. **Dr. Squatch Fireside Bourbon Cologne** — bourbon, cedarwood, a current of smoke underneath. Not loud. Not complicated. He put two passes on his neck and one on each wrist and that was it. There's a specific kind of man who enters a room and you register him a moment before he speaks, before you've consciously clocked anything about him. It's usually the smell. It settles before the voice does. He had learned this empirically and had adjusted accordingly.

---

He trained at six-thirty. An hour. He had stopped tracking the exact workouts with the fervor he'd had in his late twenties, when everything got logged and compared to last Tuesday. Now he trained to stay useful — strong enough to carry things, mobile enough to mean it, recovered enough to do it again the day after. The physical therapist had made a point, gently but without mercy, that his recovery protocol had one significant gap: his feet and his posterior chain were receiving zero attention, and this was why his lower back had its opinions.

He'd bought the **Therabody WaveRoller** the week after that appointment. Full-length vibrating foam roller, five settings. He used it after every session now — calves, IT bands, the long muscles along his spine. He'd started rolling the arches of his feet last and discovered that this, specifically, was the thing the physical therapist had meant. Something had been held there for years without knowing it was held. Different man by morning was not an overstatement. It was close to accurate.

He'd added the **Therabody Wave Solo and Roller Bundle** six weeks later because there were places the roller couldn't isolate — the knots that live in a specific inch of tissue and require pressure, not length. The Wave Solo handled those. He used the two together now as a system. Before, recovery had meant ice and the vague hope that the body would sort itself out overnight. It often didn't. Now it had a protocol, and the protocol worked, and he had stopped waiting to feel better by morning because feeling better by morning was the expected outcome.

---

He showered again. Light rinse. Dressed.

His wallet had lived in a drawer since the Ridge arrived. The folded leather thing — he'd carried it for six years, could not explain why, it had been adequate, then embarrassingly not adequate, then something he was slightly avoiding thinking about. A man had handed him the **The Ridge Wallet Royal Black** at a dinner, set it flat on the table, said *just look at it*. Aluminum plates, cash clip, nothing extraneous. Cards stacked cleanly. Sat flat in the front pocket without the silhouette of something that had been through too much. He had moved his cards over that same night. The old wallet went in the drawer and stayed there.

He put the Ridge in his pocket. Picked up his phone. Looked around the bathroom the way you do before you leave a hotel room — checking for what you might have forgotten.

He hadn't forgotten anything.

---

There is no revelation in this story. The man in it didn't change his life. He changed his shelf, adjusted his morning, gave his recovery the fifteen minutes it had always deserved. Compounded over enough days, this is actually the same thing as changing your life, but there's no moment of it you can point to. It just starts registering one day in the mirror. In how you move. In how the morning feels like something you built rather than something that happened to you.
